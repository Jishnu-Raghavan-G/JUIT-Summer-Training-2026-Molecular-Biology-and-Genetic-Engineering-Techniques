# PCR: Principle and Components

## Introduction

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) is a molecular biology technique used to amplify a specific region of DNA. It produces millions of copies of a selected DNA sequence from a very small starting amount.

PCR is widely used in molecular biology, genetic analysis, diagnostics, research, cloning, and microbial identification.

## Principle of PCR

PCR is based on repeated cycles of:

1. **Denaturation**
2. **Primer annealing**
3. **Extension**

During each cycle, the DNA template is separated into single strands, primers bind to their complementary sequences, and DNA polymerase synthesizes new DNA strands.

Repeated cycles result in exponential amplification of the target DNA region.

1. Denaturation

During denaturation, double-stranded DNA is heated so that the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases are disrupted.

This produces two single-stranded DNA templates.

A commonly used denaturation temperature is around 94–98°C, depending on the PCR system and protocol.

2. Annealing

During annealing, the temperature is lowered so that the primers can bind to their complementary sequences on the template DNA.

The annealing temperature depends mainly on the primer sequence and its melting temperature (Tm).

An unsuitable annealing temperature can result in poor amplification or non-specific products.

3. Extension

During extension, a thermostable DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to the 3′ end of the primer and synthesizes the complementary DNA strand.

A commonly used enzyme is Taq DNA polymerase.

Taq polymerase generally works efficiently around 72°C, although the recommended extension conditions depend on the polymerase being used.

Template DNA

The template DNA contains the region that needs to be amplified.

It may be obtained from:

Genomic DNA
Plasmid DNA
Purified DNA
cDNA

The template should be sufficiently pure because contaminants can inhibit PCR.

Primers

Primers are short single-stranded DNA sequences that define the region to be amplified.

Two primers are normally required:

Forward primer
Reverse primer

The primers provide the free 3′-OH group required for DNA polymerase to begin DNA synthesis.

DNA Polymerase

DNA polymerase synthesizes new DNA strands by adding nucleotides complementary to the template.

PCR requires a thermostable DNA polymerase because the reaction repeatedly reaches high temperatures during denaturation.

Taq DNA polymerase is one of the most commonly used examples.

dNTPs

The reaction contains the four deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates:

dATP
dTTP
dGTP
dCTP

These nucleotides act as the building blocks for synthesis of the new DNA strands.

Magnesium Ions

Mg²⁺ is an important cofactor for DNA polymerase activity.

The magnesium concentration can strongly influence PCR performance.

Too little Mg²⁺ may reduce amplification, while excessive Mg²⁺ can promote non-specific amplification.

Reaction Buffer

The PCR buffer maintains the chemical conditions required for DNA polymerase activity.

It helps provide an appropriate:

pH
ionic environment
salt concentration

The recommended buffer supplied with the polymerase should generally be used.

Denaturation ──┐

               ┘
        ↓
Final extension

A typical PCR may contain approximately 25–40 cycles, depending on the experiment.

The exact temperatures and times should be optimized according to the primers, template and polymerase.

Amplification

Ideally, the amount of target DNA approximately doubles during each successful PCR cycle.

The theoretical amplification can be represented as:

DNA after n cycles ≈ Initial DNA × 2ⁿ

For example:

1 cycle → approximately 2 copies
2 cycles → approximately 4 copies
3 cycles → approximately 8 copies
10 cycles → approximately 1,024 copies

In actual PCR reactions, amplification is not perfectly exponential throughout the entire reaction because reagents become limiting and amplification efficiency decreases.

Controls

Controls are important for interpreting PCR results.

Positive Control

Contains a template known to produce the expected PCR product.

It confirms that the PCR system is functioning properly.

Negative Control / No-Template Control

Contains all necessary PCR components except template DNA.

It helps identify contamination.

If a band appears in the no-template control, contamination or non-specific amplification may be present.

PCR Product

The DNA produced by PCR is called the amplicon.

The expected size of the amplicon is determined by the distance between the primer-binding sites on the template.

PCR products are commonly analysed using agarose gel electrophoresis.

Factors Affecting PCR

Important factors include:

Template DNA quality
Primer design
Primer concentration
Annealing temperature
Mg²⁺ concentration
dNTP concentration
DNA polymerase
Number of cycles
Extension conditions
Contamination

Small changes in these conditions can significantly affect amplification.

Common Problems
No PCR Product

Possible causes:

Poor-quality template
Incorrect primer sequence
Incorrect annealing temperature
Inactive polymerase
Missing reaction component
Incorrect thermal cycling conditions
Multiple Bands

Possible causes:

Non-specific primer binding
Low annealing temperature
Excessive template or primers
Poor primer design
Very Faint Band

Possible causes:

Low template concentration
Poor amplification efficiency
Insufficient number of cycles
Suboptimal reaction conditions
Band in Negative Control

This commonly suggests contamination or non-specific amplification.

Practical Workflow
Obtain suitable template DNA.
Select and prepare primers.
Prepare the PCR reaction mixture.
Place the reaction in the thermal cycler.
Perform repeated denaturation, annealing and extension cycles.
Complete the final extension.
Analyse the PCR product using agarose gel electrophoresis.
Key Takeaways
PCR amplifies a specific DNA region.
The three main stages are denaturation, annealing and extension.
Primers determine the target region.
A thermostable DNA polymerase is required.
dNTPs provide the building blocks for DNA synthesis.
Mg²⁺ is an important cofactor for polymerase activity.
Positive and negative controls help validate PCR results.
PCR products can be analysed using agarose gel electrophoresis.
