# PCR Product Analysis

## Introduction

PCR product analysis is performed to determine whether the target DNA sequence was successfully amplified and whether the resulting product has the expected size.

A common method for analysing PCR products is **agarose gel electrophoresis**.

## PCR Amplicon

The DNA fragment produced during PCR is called an **amplicon**.

Its expected length is determined by the positions of the forward and reverse primers on the template DNA.

For example, if the primers are positioned so that the target region is 500 bp long, the expected PCR product is approximately **500 bp**.

Gel Electrophoresis

PCR products are loaded into an agarose gel along with a DNA ladder.

Because DNA is negatively charged, it migrates toward the positive electrode when an electric field is applied.

Smaller DNA fragments generally migrate farther through the gel than larger fragments.

Role of DNA Ladder

The DNA ladder contains DNA fragments of known sizes.

It provides a reference for estimating the size of the PCR product.

DNA ladder          PCR sample

1000 bp ──
 700 bp ──
 500 bp ──            ──
 300 bp ──
 100 bp ──

If the sample band migrates near the 500 bp marker, the PCR product is approximately 500 bp.

Interpretation of PCR Bands
Single Band at Expected Size

A clear band at the expected size generally indicates successful amplification of the target region.

However, band size alone does not always prove that the sequence is correct.

Multiple Bands

Multiple bands may indicate:

Non-specific amplification
Poor primer specificity
Suboptimal annealing temperature
Primer-dimer formation
More than one target being amplified
No Band

Possible reasons include:

PCR failure
Poor template DNA
Incorrect primer design
Missing reaction component
Incorrect cycling conditions
Smear

A smear may indicate:

Non-specific amplification
Poor-quality template DNA
Excessive DNA loading
Inappropriate PCR conditions
Excessive number of cycles
Primer-Dimer

Primer-dimers are small DNA products formed when primers interact with each other.

They generally appear as small bands near the bottom of the gel.

Primer-dimer formation can reduce the availability of primers for amplification of the intended target.

Comparing Observed and Expected Size

The PCR product size should be compared with the expected amplicon size.

For example:

Expected product = 600 bp

Observed band ≈ 600 bp

This supports successful amplification of a product of the expected length.

If the observed size differs significantly, the PCR conditions, primer design or template should be examined.

Controls

Controls are important for reliable interpretation.

Positive Control

A known DNA template should produce the expected PCR product.

If the positive control fails, the PCR system may not be functioning properly.

No-Template Control

This contains all PCR components except template DNA.

Normally, no target-specific band should appear.

A band in the no-template control may indicate contamination.

Troubleshooting
Observation	Possible Cause

Confirmation of PCR Product

A band at the expected size provides useful evidence that amplification occurred, but additional confirmation may be required for some experiments.

Depending on the purpose, PCR products may be further analysed by:

DNA sequencing
Restriction digestion
Cloning
Specific molecular assays
Practical Workflow
Complete the PCR reaction.
Prepare an agarose gel.
Mix PCR products with loading dye if required.
Load the DNA ladder and samples.
Run electrophoresis under appropriate conditions.
Visualize the DNA bands.
Compare the sample bands with the DNA ladder.
Determine whether the expected amplicon is present.
Investigate unexpected results using appropriate troubleshooting steps.
Key Takeaways
PCR products are called amplicons.
Agarose gel electrophoresis is commonly used to analyse PCR products.
A DNA ladder helps estimate amplicon size.
A single band at the expected size generally supports successful amplification.
Multiple bands may indicate non-specific amplification.
A band in the no-template control suggests possible contamination.
Further methods may be needed to confirm the identity of a PCR product.
