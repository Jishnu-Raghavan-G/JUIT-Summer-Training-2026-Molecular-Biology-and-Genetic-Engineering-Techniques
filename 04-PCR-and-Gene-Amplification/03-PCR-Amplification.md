# PCR Amplification

## Introduction

PCR amplification is the practical process of producing many copies of a selected DNA region using the Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR).

It uses a DNA template, primers, nucleotides, DNA polymerase and controlled temperature cycles to selectively amplify the target sequence.

## Principle

PCR amplification occurs through repeated cycles of three main steps:

1. **Denaturation**
2. **Annealing**
3. **Extension**

Each successful cycle generates additional copies of the target DNA, resulting in rapid amplification over multiple cycles.

## Components of the PCR Reaction

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| Template DNA | Contains the target sequence |
| Forward primer | Binds to one side of the target |
| Reverse primer | Binds to the opposite side |
| DNA polymerase | Synthesizes new DNA |
| dNTPs | Building blocks for DNA synthesis |
| Mg²⁺ | Cofactor required by DNA polymerase |
| PCR buffer | Provides suitable reaction conditions |
| Nuclease-free water | Adjusts the final reaction volume |

## PCR Amplification Cycle

### 1. Denaturation

The reaction is heated to separate double-stranded DNA into two single strands.

Typical range:

**94–98°C**

### 2. Annealing

The temperature is lowered so that the primers bind to their complementary target sequences.

The annealing temperature depends mainly on the primer melting temperature.

### 3. Extension

DNA polymerase extends the primers by adding complementary dNTPs.

For many conventional Taq polymerase reactions, extension occurs around:

**72°C**

The exact conditions depend on the polymerase and protocol.

A typical PCR may use approximately 25–40 cycles, depending on the amount of starting template and the desired product yield.

General PCR Procedure
Prepare the PCR reaction components.
Thaw reagents according to laboratory practice and mix them properly.
Prepare a master mix when multiple reactions are being performed.
Add the required template DNA separately where appropriate.
Include positive and negative controls.
Place the tubes in the thermal cycler.
Program the required temperatures and times.
Start the PCR reaction.
After amplification, analyse the PCR product using agarose gel electrophoresis.
Master Mix

When several PCR reactions are performed, a master mix can be prepared containing common components such as:

PCR buffer
dNTPs
Mg²⁺, if not already included
DNA polymerase
Primers, depending on the experimental design

The master mix reduces pipetting variation and improves consistency between reactions.

PCR Controls
Positive Control

Contains a template known to produce the expected PCR product.

It confirms that the PCR reagents and cycling conditions are functioning properly.

No-Template Control

Contains all reaction components except template DNA.

It helps detect contamination.

Positive control → Expected band should appear

Sample           → Target band expected

No-template      → No band should appear
PCR Product

The amplified DNA fragment is called the amplicon.

Its size is determined by the positions of the forward and reverse primers on the original template.

The PCR product can be analysed using agarose gel electrophoresis.

PCR Amplification and Exponential Growth

Ideally, the amount of target DNA approximately doubles during each successful cycle.

The theoretical relationship is:

N = N₀ × 2ⁿ

Where:

N = DNA molecules after amplification
N₀ = initial number of DNA molecules
n = number of cycles

For example:

10 cycles → approximately 2¹⁰ = 1,024-fold theoretical amplification

In an actual reaction, amplification eventually slows because reagents become limiting and reaction efficiency decreases.

Common Problems
No Amplification

Possible causes:

Missing PCR component
Poor-quality template
Incorrect primer sequence
Incorrect annealing temperature
Inactive polymerase
Incorrect thermal cycling program
Multiple Bands

Possible causes:

Non-specific primer binding
Low annealing temperature
Poor primer design
Excessive template or primer concentration
Faint PCR Product

Possible causes:

Low template concentration
Poor amplification efficiency
Suboptimal reaction conditions
Insufficient amplification
Contamination

A band in the no-template control may indicate contamination of reagents, equipment or samples.

Analysis of PCR Products

The expected result is usually a band at the approximate size of the target amplicon.

Key Takeaways
PCR selectively amplifies a specific region of DNA.
