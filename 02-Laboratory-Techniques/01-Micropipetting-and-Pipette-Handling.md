Micropipetting and Pipette Handling
Introduction

Micropipetting is one of the most frequently used skills in a molecular biology laboratory. Almost every experiment involving DNA, RNA, proteins, enzymes, buffers, PCR mixtures, or bacterial cultures requires the transfer of relatively small liquid volumes.

Unlike transferring liquids with a measuring cylinder or serological pipette, micropipettes are designed for accurate handling of volumes generally ranging from the microlitre range to a few millilitres, depending on the instrument.

During molecular biology practical work, even a small pipetting error can change the concentration of a reagent and consequently affect the entire experiment. For this reason, correct pipette selection, volume setting, tip handling, aspiration, dispensing, and contamination control are essential laboratory skills.

1. What is a Micropipette?

A micropipette is a precision laboratory instrument used to measure and transfer small volumes of liquid.

The volume is usually expressed in:

[
\mu L
]

where:

[
1,\mu L=10^{-6},L
]

and:

[
1000,\mu L=1,mL
]

Micropipettes are commonly used for:

DNA samples
RNA samples
primers
PCR reagents
restriction enzymes
buffers
protein samples
bacterial suspensions
staining solutions
assay reagents
2. Common Micropipette Ranges

Different pipettes are designed for different volume ranges.

Typical examples include:

Pipette	Approximate working range
P10	0.5–10 µL
P20	2–20 µL
P200	20–200 µL
P1000	100–1000 µL

The exact operating range depends on the specific pipette model.

A pipette should not be used outside its specified range.

For example, if a volume of 5 µL is required, a P1000 would be a poor choice even though it may technically be able to dispense liquid. A smaller pipette designed for that range will generally provide better accuracy and precision.

3. Accuracy and Precision

Two terms are particularly important when discussing pipetting.

Accuracy

Accuracy describes how close the measured volume is to the true or intended volume.

Precision

Precision describes how close repeated measurements are to one another.

A pipette can therefore be:

accurate and precise
accurate but imprecise
precise but inaccurate
neither accurate nor precise

For molecular biology, both properties are important.

5. Setting the Volume

The required volume should be set using the pipette's adjustment mechanism.

The volume display must be read according to the scale used by that particular pipette.

For example, the display arrangement of a P20 is not interpreted in exactly the same way as that of a P1000.

Therefore, before using an unfamiliar pipette, it is important to check:

the pipette model
its volume range
the meaning of the display
the manufacturer's operating instructions

Forcing the adjustment mechanism beyond its specified range can damage the instrument.

6. Selecting the Correct Pipette

The first decision should always be:

What volume needs to be transferred?

Then select the smallest appropriate pipette whose operating range includes that volume.

For example:

Required volume	Suitable choice
5 µL	P10/P20 depending on specified range
15 µL	P20
100 µL	P200
500 µL	P1000

The exact choice should always follow the pipette's stated operating range.

7. Pipette Tips

Micropipettes use disposable tips that come into direct contact with the liquid.

Tips are available in different sizes and formats corresponding to different pipettes.

Important considerations include:

correct tip size
proper attachment
cleanliness
compatibility with the pipette
sterility when required

For molecular biology, sterile tips are commonly used when contamination could affect the experiment.

8. Attaching a Pipette Tip

A tip should be attached securely without excessive force.

The pipette is normally positioned vertically or appropriately aligned with the tip rack, and the shaft is pressed into the tip sufficiently to form a proper seal.

The tip should not be repeatedly forced against the rack because excessive mechanical force can affect the pipette cone.

Once attached, the tip should remain clean and should not touch laboratory surfaces unnecessarily.

9. The Two Plunger Stops

Many adjustable micropipettes have two distinct plunger positions.

First stop

Used during normal aspiration and dispensing of the selected volume.

Second stop

Used to expel the remaining liquid from the tip during dispensing.

A simplified representation is:

Plunger released
      ↓
   ───────

First stop
      ↓
   ───────

Second stop
      ↓
   ───────

Understanding the difference between the two stops is essential for accurate pipetting.

10. Basic Aspiration Procedure

A general micropipetting sequence is:

Select pipette
      ↓
Set required volume
      ↓
Attach appropriate tip
      ↓
Press plunger to first stop
      ↓
Immerse tip appropriately
      ↓
Release plunger slowly
      ↓
Withdraw tip
      ↓
Dispense into receiving vessel
      ↓
Press to first stop
      ↓
Press to second stop
      ↓
Eject tip

The exact procedure may vary slightly depending on the instrument and liquid.

11. Aspirating Liquid Correctly

When aspirating:

Press the plunger to the first stop.
Place the tip into the liquid to an appropriate depth.
Keep the pipette reasonably vertical.
Release the plunger slowly and smoothly.
Allow the liquid to enter the tip.
Pause briefly if appropriate.
Withdraw the tip without touching the outside of the vessel.

The plunger should not be released suddenly.

Rapid aspiration can produce:

bubbles
splashing
aerosols
inaccurate volume transfer
12. Tip Immersion Depth

The tip should not normally be immersed excessively deep into the liquid.

If the tip is too deep:

external liquid can adhere to the tip
the apparent transferred volume may be affected
contamination risk can increase

If the tip is barely touching the liquid:

air may be aspirated
incomplete aspiration may occur

The appropriate immersion depth depends on the pipette and volume being transferred.

13. Dispensing Liquid

After aspiration, the receiving vessel should be positioned appropriately.

A general dispensing sequence is:

Place the tip against or near the appropriate area of the receiving vessel.
Press the plunger smoothly to the first stop.
Pause briefly.
Press to the second stop to expel remaining liquid.
Withdraw the tip appropriately.
Eject the used tip.

The exact contact technique depends on the liquid and protocol.

14. Avoiding Air Bubbles

Air bubbles inside the tip can lead to inaccurate volume delivery.

They may occur because of:

rapid aspiration
incorrect tip immersion
releasing the plunger too quickly
improper pipetting technique
inappropriate handling of volatile liquids

If a visible bubble is present when accuracy matters, the sample should generally be aspirated again rather than assuming the volume is correct.

15. Reverse Pipetting

Reverse pipetting is useful for certain liquids, especially:

viscous liquids
foaming liquids
liquids that do not dispense cleanly using standard technique

In reverse pipetting, the pipette draws more liquid than the nominal selected volume and the excess is retained in the tip after dispensing.

The technique is useful when complete dispensing of the selected volume is difficult.

It should be used only when appropriate for the liquid and pipette.

16. Pipetting Viscous Liquids

Viscous liquids behave differently from water-like solutions.

Examples can include:

glycerol-containing solutions
concentrated protein solutions
some detergents
highly concentrated reagents

For such liquids:

aspiration should be slow
dispensing should be controlled
sufficient time may be allowed for the liquid to enter the tip
reverse pipetting may improve consistency

The pipette should not be treated as though every liquid has the same physical properties.

17. Pipetting Volatile Liquids

Volatile liquids can evaporate inside the tip and may produce changes in the delivered volume.

Factors such as:

temperature
vapor pressure
aspiration speed
equilibration

can influence performance.

For sensitive applications, specialized pipetting techniques may be required.

18. Pre-Wetting the Tip

For some applications, pre-wetting the tip can improve consistency.

This involves aspirating and dispensing the liquid one or more times before the actual transfer.

Pre-wetting can help reduce effects associated with:

liquid retention
evaporation
temperature differences
surface tension

It is particularly useful when transferring small volumes or liquids whose physical properties differ substantially from water.

The appropriate technique depends on the protocol.

19. Pipetting Small Volumes

Small-volume transfers require greater care.

For example, transferring:

[
2,\mu L
]

is much more sensitive to technique than transferring:

[
200,\mu L
]

Small-volume errors can arise from:

poor tip handling
evaporation
incorrect immersion
bubbles
pipette limitations
inconsistent plunger movement

Therefore, selecting a suitable pipette is particularly important for small-volume work.

20. Example: Preparing a PCR Reaction

Suppose a PCR reaction requires:

Component	Volume
Master mix	12.5 µL
Forward primer	1 µL
Reverse primer	1 µL
Template DNA	2 µL
Nuclease-free water	8.5 µL
Total	25 µL

The total volume is:

[
12.5+1+1+2+8.5=25,\mu L
]

Therefore:

[
\boxed{V_{total}=25,\mu L}
]

The pipette used for each component should be selected according to the individual transfer volume.

For example, a 1 µL transfer should not automatically be performed using a large-volume pipette merely because the liquid volume is small enough to be physically aspirated.

21. Pipetting and Dilution Accuracy

Consider a DNA sample with:

[
C_1=200,ng/\mu L
]

Suppose 25 µL of sample is diluted to a final volume of 100 µL.

The dilution factor is:

[
DF=\frac{100}{25}=4
]

Therefore:

[
C_2=\frac{200}{4}
]

[
C_2=50,ng/\mu L
]

A pipetting error in either the 25 µL sample volume or the final volume would change the actual concentration.

This demonstrates why pipetting accuracy directly affects experimental calculations.

22. Serial Dilution and Pipetting

Suppose a sample is diluted 1:10 three consecutive times.

First dilution:

[
1:10
]

Second dilution:

[
1:10\times1:10=1:100
]

Third dilution:

[
1:100\times1:10=1:1000
]

Therefore:

[
\boxed{DF=1000}
]

A small error during each transfer can accumulate across the series.

For this reason, serial dilution experiments require consistent technique.

23. Preventing Cross-Contamination

Cross-contamination is one of the most important concerns in molecular biology.

A used tip should generally be discarded rather than reused when working with different samples or reagents.

Good practice includes:

changing tips between samples
changing tips between reagents when required
avoiding contact between the tip and external surfaces
keeping reagent bottles closed
using sterile tips for contamination-sensitive procedures
separating pre-PCR and post-PCR work when appropriate
24. Aerosol Contamination

Some molecular biology procedures, particularly PCR, are highly sensitive to contamination.

Pipetting can generate aerosols, especially when liquids are aspirated or dispensed rapidly.

For sensitive applications, filter/aerosol-resistant tips may be used.

Good pipetting technique reduces the chance of aerosol formation.

25. Pipetting Order in Molecular Biology

When preparing reactions containing multiple components, the order of addition may matter.

A common general strategy is:

water or diluent
reaction buffer/master mix
primers or other reagents
enzyme
template

The exact order should follow the validated protocol.

Highly concentrated or contamination-sensitive components should be handled carefully.

26. Enzyme Pipetting

Restriction enzymes, DNA polymerases, ligases, and other enzymes may be supplied in small volumes and concentrated formulations.

Good practice includes:

keeping enzymes under their recommended storage conditions
minimizing unnecessary time outside controlled storage
mixing gently when appropriate
avoiding repeated unnecessary freeze-thaw cycles
using clean tips
changing tips between reagents
avoiding contamination of the stock

Enzymes should not be handled casually simply because only a few microlitres are required.

27. Pipetting DNA and RNA

Nucleic-acid samples can also be affected by poor handling.

For DNA:

avoid unnecessary contamination
use appropriate tips
mix samples consistently
avoid excessive mechanical treatment when the application is sensitive to DNA fragmentation

For RNA:

RNase contamination is a particularly important concern
use RNase-free consumables when required
maintain appropriate clean handling practices
28. Pipette Contamination

Contamination can potentially enter the pipette if liquid is accidentally aspirated beyond the tip.

This may happen because of:

over-aspiration
excessive immersion
rapid plunger release
incorrect pipetting angle
unsuitable technique

If liquid enters the pipette shaft, the instrument may require inspection and appropriate cleaning or servicing according to the manufacturer's instructions.

The pipette should not simply be returned to normal use without addressing the contamination risk.

29. Pipette Calibration

Micropipettes require periodic calibration and performance verification.

Calibration checks whether the pipette delivers volumes within acceptable limits.

A common gravimetric principle uses water and a calibrated balance.

The mass of water can be related to volume using water density:

[
V=\frac{m}{\rho}
]

where:

(V) = volume
(m) = measured mass
(\rho) = density of water under the measurement conditions

For example, approximately:

[
1,g\ water\approx1,mL
]

near room temperature, although the exact relationship depends on temperature.

Calibration is normally performed according to established laboratory procedures rather than by informal estimation.

30. Accuracy Versus Relative Error

Suppose the intended volume is:

[
100,\mu L
]

but the measured volume is:

[
98,\mu L
]

The absolute error is:

[
98-100=-2,\mu L
]

The magnitude of the absolute error is:

[
2,\mu L
]

Relative error can be calculated as:

[
\text{Relative error}=
\frac{|\text{measured}-\text{true}|}{\text{true}}
\times100
]

Therefore:

[
\frac{|98-100|}{100}\times100
]

[
=2%
]

So the measured volume has a relative error of:

[
\boxed{2%}
]

31. Good Micropipetting Habits

The following habits improve consistency:

choose the correct pipette
stay within the manufacturer's volume range
use compatible tips
set the volume carefully
hold the pipette appropriately
aspirate slowly
dispense smoothly
avoid bubbles
maintain consistent immersion depth
change tips when required
keep the tip away from contaminated surfaces
close reagent containers promptly
return the pipette to its recommended storage position

Consistency is often more important than simply performing the movement quickly.

32. Common Pipetting Mistakes
Mistake 1: Using the wrong pipette

A volume may technically fit within a large pipette's range but still be better handled using a smaller suitable pipette.

Mistake 2: Setting the volume beyond the range

This can damage the pipette.

Mistake 3: Releasing the plunger too quickly

This can introduce bubbles and aerosols.

Mistake 4: Immersing the tip too deeply

This can affect aspiration and contaminate the external tip surface.

Mistake 5: Touching the tip to laboratory surfaces

This can introduce contamination.

Mistake 6: Reusing a contaminated tip

This can transfer material between samples.

Mistake 7: Ignoring liquid properties

Water, glycerol-rich solutions, detergents, and viscous samples do not necessarily behave identically.

Mistake 8: Treating every pipetting error as negligible

A small error can become significant when the transferred volume is very small or when several dilution steps are involved.

34. Why Micropipetting Is a Critical Molecular Biology Skill

Micropipetting may look simple compared with techniques such as PCR, DNA extraction, or SDS-PAGE, but it forms the basis of all of them.

A PCR reaction is only as reliable as the volumes and concentrations of the components added to it.

Similarly:

incorrect DNA volume affects template concentration
incorrect primer volume affects primer concentration
incorrect enzyme volume affects reaction conditions
incorrect buffer volume changes the reaction environment
inconsistent sample volumes reduce reproducibility

Therefore, micropipetting should be treated as a quantitative experimental technique rather than simply transferring liquid from one tube to another.

Learning Note

Micropipetting is one of those laboratory skills that initially appears very straightforward, but accuracy depends on many small details. The choice of pipette, tip, aspiration speed, immersion depth, plunger control, and handling of the receiving tube can all influence the final volume.

The connection with molecular biology became particularly clear when considering PCR and dilution calculations. A difference of only a fraction of a microlitre can matter when working with concentrated primers or enzymes. Developing consistent pipetting habits is therefore not just about becoming comfortable with the instrument; it is part of maintaining the quantitative reliability of the entire experiment.
