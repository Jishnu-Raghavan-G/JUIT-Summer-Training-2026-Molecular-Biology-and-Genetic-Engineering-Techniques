# Centrifugation and Sample Handling

## Introduction

Centrifugation is a routine laboratory technique used to separate components of a sample according to differences in their sedimentation behavior. It is widely used in molecular biology for collecting cells, separating cellular components, recovering DNA or protein-containing fractions, and removing unwanted particles from solutions.

Correct sample handling before, during, and after centrifugation is important for obtaining reproducible results and preventing sample loss or contamination.

---

## 1. Principle of Centrifugation

Centrifugation uses centrifugal force to separate particles suspended in a liquid.

When a sample is centrifuged:

* Denser or larger particles generally sediment faster.
* Less dense components remain closer to the liquid phase.
* The material collected at the bottom is called the **pellet**.
* The liquid remaining above the pellet is called the **supernatant**.

A simplified representation is:

```text
        Centrifuge tube
       ________________
      |                |
      |   Supernatant  |
      |                |
      |----------------|
      |     Pellet     |
      |________________|
```

---

## 2. Relative Centrifugal Force (RCF)

Centrifugation is commonly expressed using **relative centrifugal force (RCF)**, also called ×g.

RCF depends on:

* rotational speed (RPM)
* radius of the centrifuge rotor

The relationship is:

$$
RCF = 1.118\times10^{-5}\times r\times RPM^2
$$

where:

* \(r\) = radius in centimetres
* RPM = revolutions per minute

Therefore, RPM values from different centrifuges cannot always be directly compared because the rotor radius may be different.

---

## 3. RPM vs RCF

### RPM

RPM describes how fast the rotor is spinning.

### RCF

RCF describes the actual relative centrifugal force experienced by the sample.

For reproducible biological experiments, protocols often specify **×g** rather than only RPM.

---

## 4. Main Components of a Centrifuge

A laboratory centrifuge generally contains:

* rotor
* centrifuge tubes
* tube holders or adapters
* motor
* speed control
* timer
* lid and safety-lock mechanism
* display/control panel

Different rotors may be designed for different tube sizes and applications.

---

## 5. Balancing the Centrifuge

Proper balancing is one of the most important centrifuge precautions.

Tubes should be placed opposite each other with equal or appropriately matched masses.

For example:

```text
        Tube
          |
     \    |    /
      \   |   /
       \  |  /
        Rotor
       /     \
      /       \
     Tube    Tube
```

An unbalanced rotor can cause:

* strong vibration
* noise
* damage to the centrifuge
* tube breakage
* sample loss
* safety hazards

Never operate a centrifuge with an obviously unbalanced load.

---

## 6. General Centrifugation Procedure

A basic workflow is:

1. Select appropriate centrifuge tubes.
2. Transfer the samples carefully.
3. Check tube compatibility with the rotor.
4. Balance the tubes properly.
5. Place the tubes securely in the rotor.
6. Close the centrifuge lid.
7. Set the required RCF/RPM and time according to the protocol.
8. Start the centrifuge.
9. Wait until the rotor has completely stopped.
10. Remove the tubes carefully.
11. Collect the pellet or supernatant as required.

---

## 7. Pellet and Supernatant Handling

After centrifugation, the pellet may be loosely or firmly attached to the bottom of the tube.

When removing the supernatant:

* avoid disturbing the pellet
* aspirate slowly
* keep the pipette tip away from the pellet
* leave a small amount of liquid if necessary rather than accidentally removing the pellet

If the pellet is required for further processing, it should be resuspended carefully in the appropriate buffer.

---

## 8. Sample Handling During Centrifugation

Good sample handling includes:

* keeping samples properly labelled
* using the correct tube type
* avoiding unnecessary repeated transfers
* minimizing contamination
* maintaining appropriate temperature when required
* processing samples within the recommended time

For temperature-sensitive samples, a refrigerated centrifuge may be required.

---

## 9. Common Applications in Molecular Biology

Centrifugation is used in many procedures.

| Application                 | Purpose                                                     |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cell harvesting             | Collecting cells from culture                               |
| DNA isolation               | Separating cellular debris and DNA-containing fractions     |
| RNA preparation             | Removing insoluble material and separating phases/fractions |
| Protein extraction          | Clarifying cell lysates                                     |
| Bacterial transformation    | Collecting cells during washing or recovery steps           |
| PCR preparation             | Briefly collecting liquid from tube walls/caps              |
| Gel-related sample handling | Collecting recovered material after processing              |

---

## 10. Short Centrifugation Steps During Pipetting

A brief centrifugation, sometimes called a **quick spin**, is often used to bring liquid from the sides or cap of a microcentrifuge tube to the bottom.

This is useful when:

* droplets are present on the tube wall
* liquid is trapped in the cap
* reagents need to be collected at the bottom before mixing or pipetting

The exact speed and duration depend on the equipment and experimental protocol.

---

## 11. Important Precautions

* Always inspect tubes before use.
* Do not use cracked or damaged tubes.
* Ensure tubes are compatible with the selected rotor.
* Balance samples properly.
* Do not open the centrifuge while the rotor is spinning.
* Wait for complete stopping before opening the lid.
* Do not exceed the tube or rotor's rated limits.
* Keep the centrifuge clean and dry.
* Follow the manufacturer's operating instructions.

---

## 12. Common Errors

### Improper balancing

This can produce excessive vibration and may damage the instrument.

### Disturbing the pellet

Rapid aspiration can cause the pellet to become suspended again.

### Using incompatible tubes

Some tubes cannot withstand high centrifugal forces.

### Confusing RPM with RCF

The same RPM does not necessarily produce the same RCF on different centrifuges.

### Opening before complete stopping

This creates a safety risk and should never be done.

---

## 13. Practical Sample-Handling Checklist

Before centrifugation:

* [ ] Samples are correctly labelled.
* [ ] Tubes are suitable for the centrifuge.
* [ ] Tubes are properly balanced.
* [ ] Rotor is correctly installed.
* [ ] Required RCF/RPM and time are known.

After centrifugation:

* [ ] Rotor has completely stopped.
* [ ] Tubes are removed carefully.
* [ ] Pellet position is identified.
* [ ] Supernatant is removed without disturbing the pellet.
* [ ] Samples are processed or stored as required.

---

## Key Takeaways

* Centrifugation separates sample components using centrifugal force.
* The **pellet** forms at the bottom while the **supernatant** remains above it.
* RCF (×g) is generally more useful for reproducing centrifugation conditions than RPM alone.
* Proper balancing is essential for safe centrifuge operation.
* Careful removal of the supernatant prevents loss of the desired pellet.
* Centrifugation is involved in many DNA, RNA, protein, and microbiological procedures.

## Learning Note

During laboratory work, I observed that centrifugation is not simply a step of placing tubes into a machine. Correct balancing, selection of centrifugation conditions, and careful handling of the pellet and supernatant directly affect the quality of the sample obtained for subsequent experiments.
