# DNA, RNA and Protein Structure

## 1. Introduction

DNA, RNA and proteins are three of the most important classes of biological macromolecules. Understanding their structures is essential for understanding almost every molecular biology technique used during the training programme.

The structure of a molecule determines how it behaves. For example, the complementary nature of DNA strands makes accurate copying possible, the chemical difference between DNA and RNA affects their stability, and the three-dimensional structure of proteins determines their function.

The three molecules can be broadly connected as:

```text
DNA
 ↓ transcription
RNA
 ↓ translation
Protein
```

However, each molecule has its own composition, structural organization and biological role.

---

# 2. Nucleic Acids

DNA and RNA belong to a group of biomolecules called **nucleic acids**.

Both are polymers made up of smaller units called **nucleotides**.

A nucleotide consists of:

```text
Nucleotide
│
├── Nitrogenous base
├── Pentose sugar
└── Phosphate group
```

When a nucleic acid contains a sugar and a nitrogenous base but no phosphate group, it is called a **nucleoside**.

Therefore:

```text
Nucleoside = Sugar + Base

Nucleotide = Sugar + Base + Phosphate
```

This distinction is small but important when studying nucleic-acid structure.

---

# 3. Nitrogenous Bases

The bases found in nucleic acids are divided into two groups.

## Purines

Purines have a two-ring structure.

* Adenine (A)
* Guanine (G)

## Pyrimidines

Pyrimidines have a single-ring structure.

* Cytosine (C)
* Thymine (T)
* Uracil (U)

The distribution in the two major nucleic acids is:

| Base     | DNA | RNA |
| -------- | --: | --: |
| Adenine  |   ✓ |   ✓ |
| Guanine  |   ✓ |   ✓ |
| Cytosine |   ✓ |   ✓ |
| Thymine  |   ✓ |   — |
| Uracil   |   — |   ✓ |

Thus, the major base difference between DNA and RNA is:

```text
DNA → Thymine
RNA → Uracil
```

---

# 4. DNA Nucleotide

A DNA nucleotide contains:

```text
Phosphate
    │
    ↓
Deoxyribose sugar
    │
    ↓
Nitrogenous base
```

The sugar is **2-deoxyribose**.

The term "deoxy" refers to the absence of an oxygen atom at the 2′ carbon compared with ribose.

This apparently small structural difference contributes to the different chemical properties of DNA and RNA.

---

# 5. RNA Nucleotide

RNA nucleotides contain:

* Ribose sugar
* Phosphate group
* Nitrogenous base

Ribose contains a hydroxyl group at the 2′ carbon:

```text
RNA → 2′-OH present
DNA → 2′-OH absent
```

The additional 2′-OH group makes RNA chemically more susceptible to hydrolysis than DNA under appropriate conditions.

---

# 6. Phosphodiester Bonds

Nucleotides are joined together through **phosphodiester bonds**.

The phosphate group connects the sugar of one nucleotide to the sugar of the next nucleotide.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Sugar — Phosphate — Sugar — Phosphate — Sugar
   │                    │
 Base                 Base
```

This creates the repeating **sugar-phosphate backbone** of the nucleic acid.

The bases project from this backbone and carry the sequence information.

---

# 7. 5′ and 3′ Ends

Nucleic acids have directionality.

The two ends of a strand are referred to as:

* **5′ end**
* **3′ end**

These names arise from the carbon numbering of the sugar.

A simplified strand can be represented as:

```text
5′ ─────────────── 3′
```

DNA and RNA polymerases synthesize nucleic acids by adding nucleotides to the **3′ hydroxyl group**.

Therefore, newly synthesized nucleic acids grow in the:

```text
5′ → 3′ direction
```

This is a fundamental concept for understanding DNA replication, PCR and transcription.

---

# 8. DNA Double-Stranded Structure

DNA commonly exists as two complementary strands arranged into a double helix.

The strands are **antiparallel**:

```text
5′ ───────────────── 3′
3′ ───────────────── 5′
```

The bases of the two strands interact through complementary base pairing.

```text
A ↔ T
G ↔ C
```

Therefore, if one DNA strand is:

```text
5′-ATGCCAT-3′
```

the complementary strand is:

```text
3′-TACGGTA-5′
```

If the complementary strand is written in the conventional 5′ → 3′ orientation, it becomes:

```text
5′-TACGGTA-3′
```

The direction should always be specified when writing DNA sequences because reversing a sequence without considering its orientation can lead to incorrect interpretation.

---

# 9. Hydrogen Bonding in DNA

Complementary bases are held together by hydrogen bonds.

The standard base pairs are:

```text
A — T
G — C
```

Adenine and thymine form **two hydrogen bonds**, while guanine and cytosine form **three hydrogen bonds**.

Therefore, DNA regions with a relatively high GC content generally have stronger base-pair interactions and can require higher temperatures to separate the strands.

This becomes particularly relevant when considering **PCR annealing and denaturation conditions**.

---

# 10. DNA Double Helix

The DNA double helix contains:

* A hydrophilic sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside
* Nitrogenous bases positioned toward the inside
* Complementary base pairing between strands
* Antiparallel orientation of the two strands

The structure allows DNA to store information while also permitting the strands to separate when necessary.

For example:

```text
DNA replication
      ↓
Strands separate
      ↓
Each strand acts as a template
      ↓
Complementary strands synthesized
```

The same basic principle of strand separation and complementary pairing is exploited in PCR.

---

# 11. Major and Minor Grooves

The DNA double helix contains two types of grooves:

* Major groove
* Minor groove

These grooves provide surfaces through which proteins can interact with DNA.

Many DNA-binding proteins recognize specific sequences by interacting with chemical groups exposed within these grooves.

Therefore, DNA is not simply an inert storage molecule. Its three-dimensional structure also allows it to interact specifically with proteins involved in replication, transcription, regulation and repair.

---

# 12. RNA Structure

RNA is usually single-stranded, but this does not mean that it has no defined structure.

An RNA strand can fold back on itself because complementary bases within the same molecule can pair.

This can produce structures such as:

* Hairpins
* Stem-loop structures
* Bulges
* Internal loops

Therefore:

```text
RNA
 ↓
Base pairing within the molecule
 ↓
Folding
 ↓
Specific secondary and tertiary structures
```

RNA structure is particularly important because some RNA molecules perform catalytic or regulatory functions.

---

# 13. DNA vs RNA

| Property             | DNA                         | RNA                                                             |
| -------------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sugar                | 2-deoxyribose               | Ribose                                                          |
| 2′-OH group          | Absent                      | Present                                                         |
| Bases                | A, T, G, C                  | A, U, G, C                                                      |
| Typical structure    | Double-stranded             | Usually single-stranded                                         |
| Chemical stability   | Relatively high             | Generally lower                                                 |
| Main biological role | Genetic information storage | Information transfer, regulation and diverse cellular functions |

These differences explain why DNA and RNA are handled differently in the laboratory.

RNA is generally more susceptible to degradation, so maintaining an RNase-free environment is especially important when working with RNA samples.

---

# 14. Complementary Base Pairing

Complementarity is one of the most useful concepts in molecular biology.

For DNA:

```text
A pairs with T
G pairs with C
```

For RNA:

```text
A pairs with U
G pairs with C
```

This principle is responsible for:

* DNA replication
* PCR primer binding
* Transcription
* RNA folding
* Hybridization-based methods
* Many molecular identification approaches

---

# 15. Denaturation and Renaturation

DNA strands can separate when the hydrogen bonds and other stabilizing interactions holding the double helix together are disrupted.

This is known as **denaturation**.

When suitable conditions are restored, complementary strands can pair again. This process is commonly described as **renaturation** or **annealing**, depending on the context.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Double-stranded DNA
        ↓ heat / suitable conditions
Single DNA strands
        ↓ cooling
Complementary strands pair
```

This principle is directly relevant to PCR.

---

# 16. Proteins

Proteins are polymers composed of **amino acids**.

There are 20 standard amino acids commonly used in ribosomally synthesized proteins.

Each amino acid contains a common structural framework:

```text
        H
        |
H₂N — C — COOH
        |
        R
```

The `R` group differs between amino acids and determines many of their chemical properties.

At physiological pH, amino acids generally exist in ionized forms rather than simply as the neutral structure shown above.

---

# 17. Classification of Amino Acid Side Chains

Amino acids can be grouped according to the properties of their side chains.

### Nonpolar / hydrophobic

Examples:

* Glycine
* Alanine
* Valine
* Leucine
* Isoleucine

### Polar uncharged

Examples:

* Serine
* Threonine
* Asparagine
* Glutamine

### Acidic

* Aspartate
* Glutamate

### Basic

* Lysine
* Arginine
* Histidine

The chemical nature of amino-acid side chains strongly influences protein folding and interactions.

---

# 18. Peptide Bonds

Amino acids are joined through **peptide bonds**.

The carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of another amino acid, with the release of water.

Simplified:

```text
Amino acid + Amino acid
          ↓
     Peptide bond
          ↓
      Dipeptide
```

Repeated formation of peptide bonds produces a polypeptide chain.

---

# 19. Primary Protein Structure

The primary structure is the **linear sequence of amino acids** in a polypeptide.

For example:

```text
Ala — Gly — Lys — Val — Leu — ...
```

The sequence is normally written from:

```text
N-terminus → C-terminus
```

The amino-acid sequence contains the information that contributes to the eventual three-dimensional structure of the protein.

---

# 20. Secondary Structure

Secondary structure refers to local folding patterns in a polypeptide chain.

Two major forms are:

### α-Helix

A coiled structure stabilized largely by hydrogen bonding within the peptide backbone.

### β-Sheet

A sheet-like arrangement formed by hydrogen bonding between segments of the polypeptide chain.

These structures are not determined simply by one type of bond; the overall sequence and chemical environment influence how a protein folds.

---

# 21. Tertiary Structure

Tertiary structure refers to the overall three-dimensional arrangement of a single polypeptide chain.

It can be stabilized by different interactions, including:

* Hydrogen bonds
* Ionic interactions
* Hydrophobic interactions
* Van der Waals interactions
* Disulfide bonds in appropriate proteins

The resulting three-dimensional structure is closely related to protein function.

---

# 22. Quaternary Structure

Some proteins contain more than one polypeptide chain.

The arrangement of these individual subunits is called **quaternary structure**.

For example:

```text
Subunit A + Subunit B
          ↓
      Protein complex
```

Not every protein has quaternary structure.

---

# 23. Protein Denaturation

Protein denaturation refers to disruption of the native higher-order structure of a protein without necessarily breaking all of its peptide bonds.

Factors that can cause denaturation include:

* Heat
* Extreme pH
* Certain chemicals
* Detergents
* Other environmental conditions

This concept is important for understanding **SDS-PAGE**.

SDS treatment helps disrupt many non-covalent interactions and gives proteins a strong negative charge. Under reducing and heating conditions commonly used for SDS-PAGE, additional structural disruption can occur.

As a result, proteins can be analysed mainly according to their molecular size.

---

# 24. DNA, RNA and Protein: A Practical Connection

The structures of these molecules explain why different laboratory techniques are required.

### DNA

Because DNA is a stable nucleic acid capable of being copied and manipulated:

```text
DNA
 ↓
Isolation
 ↓
Quantification
 ↓
PCR / Restriction digestion / Ligation
 ↓
Electrophoresis
```

### RNA

Because RNA is generally less stable and more susceptible to degradation:

```text
RNA
 ↓
Careful isolation
 ↓
Quality assessment
 ↓
Electrophoretic analysis
```

### Protein

Because proteins have complex three-dimensional structures:

```text
Protein
 ↓
Extraction
 ↓
Quantification
 ↓
Denaturation
 ↓
SDS-PAGE
 ↓
Band analysis
```

The laboratory workflow therefore follows from the chemical properties of the molecules themselves.

# 25. Important Calculations and Relationships

## 25.1 DNA Concentration from A260

For purified double-stranded DNA, a commonly used conversion is:

```text
1 A260 ≈ 50 µg/mL dsDNA
```

Therefore:

```text
DNA concentration = A260 × 50 µg/mL
```

If the sample was diluted before measurement:

```text
Original concentration
= measured concentration × dilution factor
```

### Example

Suppose:

```text
A260 = 0.20
```

Then:

```text
DNA concentration
= 0.20 × 50
= 10 µg/mL
```

Since:

```text
1 µg/mL = 1 ng/µL
```

the concentration is:

```text
10 ng/µL
```

If the original sample had been diluted 1:10:

```text
Original concentration
= 10 × 10
= 100 ng/µL
```

---

## 25.2 DNA Purity

Two commonly examined absorbance ratios are:

```text
A260/A280
A260/A230
```

For relatively pure DNA, an A260/A280 ratio around **1.8** is often considered a useful reference point.

For A260/A230, values around **2.0–2.2** are commonly associated with relatively clean nucleic-acid preparations.

These values are not absolute pass/fail criteria. They can be affected by sample composition, concentration, instrument characteristics and contaminants.

Therefore, purity ratios should be considered together with the appearance of the sample and its performance in downstream experiments.

---

## 25.3 Dilution Calculation

A very common laboratory calculation is:

```text
C₁V₁ = C₂V₂
```

where:

* `C₁` = initial concentration
* `V₁` = volume taken from the stock
* `C₂` = desired concentration
* `V₂` = final volume

For example, to prepare 200 µL of a 25 ng/µL DNA sample from a 100 ng/µL stock:

```text
100 × V₁ = 25 × 200

V₁ = 50 µL
```

Therefore:

```text
DNA stock = 50 µL
Diluent = 150 µL
Final volume = 200 µL
```

# 26. Molecular Weight and Protein Analysis

For proteins analysed using SDS-PAGE, molecular weight is generally expressed in:

```text
Da — Dalton
kDa — kilodalton
```

where:

```text
1 kDa = 1000 Da
```

Protein molecular-weight markers contain proteins of known approximate molecular weights.

The migration of an unknown protein band can be compared with these standards.

A common analysis involves plotting:

```text
log₁₀(Molecular weight)
```

against a measure of electrophoretic migration, often expressed as relative mobility.

Relative mobility can be calculated as:

```text
Rf = distance migrated by protein band
     ────────────────────────────────
     distance migrated by tracking front
```

A standard curve can then be generated from the known marker bands.

For an unknown band, its migration value can be used with the calibration curve to estimate its molecular weight.

The exact relationship is empirical and depends on the gel system and experimental conditions.

# 27. Why Structure Matters in the Laboratory

A major lesson from molecular biology is that laboratory techniques work because of the physical and chemical properties of biological molecules.

For example:

```text
Complementary DNA bases
        ↓
Primer binding
        ↓
PCR amplification
```

```text
Specific DNA recognition sequence
        ↓
Restriction enzyme recognition
        ↓
DNA cleavage
```

```text
DNA fragment size
        ↓
Different migration through gel
        ↓
Electrophoretic separation
```

```text
Protein denaturation + SDS
        ↓
Size-dependent electrophoretic migration
        ↓
SDS-PAGE
```

The practical technique therefore becomes much easier to understand when the underlying molecular principle is clear.

---

# 28. Key Takeaways

* DNA and RNA are nucleotide polymers.
* A nucleotide contains a sugar, phosphate and nitrogenous base.
* DNA contains deoxyribose and thymine.
* RNA contains ribose and uracil.
* DNA strands are antiparallel and complementary.
* DNA and RNA synthesis proceeds in the 5′ → 3′ direction.
* Complementary base pairing is fundamental to replication and PCR.
* Proteins are polymers of amino acids.
* Peptide bonds connect amino acids.
* Protein structure is described at primary, secondary, tertiary and, where applicable, quaternary levels.
* Protein structure is closely related to protein function.
* SDS treatment alters protein behaviour and allows proteins to be separated according to size in SDS-PAGE.
* DNA concentration can be estimated using A260 measurements.
* DNA purity is commonly assessed using A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios.
* Dilution calculations commonly use `C₁V₁ = C₂V₂`.
* Molecular structure provides the basis for understanding the laboratory techniques used throughout this training.
