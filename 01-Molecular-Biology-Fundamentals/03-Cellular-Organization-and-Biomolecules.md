# Cellular Organization and Biomolecules

## 1. Introduction

Molecular biology does not begin with PCR tubes, gels, or enzymes. All of these laboratory techniques ultimately deal with molecules that normally exist inside living cells.

Understanding the basic organization of a cell therefore provides useful context for understanding why DNA, RNA, proteins, lipids and other biomolecules have to be isolated, separated, quantified or analysed during laboratory work.

A cell can be viewed as an organized system in which different structures perform different functions while remaining interconnected.

```text
Cell
│
├── Genetic material
│      └── DNA
│
├── RNA
│
├── Proteins
│
├── Lipids
│
├── Carbohydrates
│
└── Small molecules / ions
```

The molecular biology techniques used during the training often involve isolating one of these components from the much more complex mixture present inside a biological sample.

---

# 2. Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Living cells can broadly be divided into **prokaryotic** and **eukaryotic** cells.

## Prokaryotic Cells

Bacteria are examples of prokaryotic organisms.

Their genetic material is not enclosed within a membrane-bound nucleus. Instead, the chromosome is located in a region called the **nucleoid**.

A simplified bacterial cell can be represented as:

```text
        Bacterial Cell
   ┌─────────────────────┐
   │                     │
   │     Nucleoid        │
   │       DNA           │
   │                     │
   │     Ribosomes       │
   │                     │
   │  Cytoplasm          │
   │                     │
   └─────────────────────┘
```

Bacteria may also contain additional DNA molecules called **plasmids**.

Plasmids are particularly important in genetic engineering because they can be used as vectors for carrying foreign DNA.

---

## Eukaryotic Cells

Eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles.

Examples include:

* Animal cells
* Plant cells
* Fungal cells
* Many protists

A simplified eukaryotic cell contains:

```text
Cell
│
├── Nucleus
├── Mitochondria
├── Endoplasmic reticulum
├── Golgi apparatus
├── Ribosomes
├── Cytoplasm
└── Plasma membrane
```

The **nucleus** contains most of the genomic DNA in a typical eukaryotic cell.

---

# 3. Comparison of Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

| Feature                   | Prokaryotic cell            | Eukaryotic cell            |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Nucleus                   | Absent                      | Present                    |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Generally absent            | Present                    |
| Genetic material          | Usually circular chromosome | Usually linear chromosomes |
| Typical size              | Smaller                     | Larger                     |
| Ribosomes                 | 70S                         | 80S in cytoplasm           |
| Examples                  | Bacteria and archaea        | Animals, plants, fungi     |

These are broad distinctions and there are important exceptions and additional details in both groups.

---

# 4. The Cell Membrane

The plasma membrane forms the boundary between the cell and its surrounding environment.

It is primarily composed of a **phospholipid bilayer** containing proteins and other molecules.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Outside
  ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○
  │ │ │ │ │ │
  │ │ │ │ │ │
  ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○
Inside
```

The membrane is selectively permeable, meaning that different substances cross it with different efficiencies and often through different mechanisms.

This becomes important when cells are disrupted during processes such as DNA or protein extraction.

---

# 5. Cytoplasm

The cytoplasm contains the cellular components outside the nucleus in eukaryotic cells and includes the cytosol together with suspended structures.

Many biochemical reactions occur in this environment.

When biological material is processed in the laboratory, the objective is often to separate a desired molecule from the many other components present in the cell.

For example:

```text
Whole cell
   ↓
Cell disruption
   ↓
Complex mixture
   ↓
Separation / purification
   ↓
Desired molecule
```

This is the basic idea behind many extraction procedures.

---

# 6. Nucleus

The nucleus is a major organelle of eukaryotic cells.

It contains most of the cell's genomic DNA and provides an environment in which DNA replication and transcription can occur.

The nuclear envelope separates nuclear contents from the cytoplasm.

A simplified organization is:

```text
Nucleus
│
├── DNA
├── Chromatin
├── Nucleolus
└── Nuclear components
```

During genomic DNA isolation from eukaryotic samples, the nuclear material must ultimately be disrupted or released so that the DNA can be recovered.

---

# 7. Chromatin

In eukaryotic cells, DNA is associated with proteins, particularly histones, to form **chromatin**.

This organization allows a very long DNA molecule to be packaged within the nucleus.

A simplified representation is:

```text
DNA
 ↓
DNA + histone proteins
 ↓
Nucleosomes
 ↓
Higher-order chromatin organization
 ↓
Chromosomes
```

Chromatin organization is also involved in regulating access to DNA.

---

# 8. Mitochondria

Mitochondria are organelles involved in cellular energy metabolism.

They contain their own DNA, known as **mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)**.

This is an important example of genetic material existing outside the nucleus in eukaryotic cells.

The presence of mitochondrial DNA is also relevant in molecular biology and genetic studies.

---

# 9. Ribosomes

Ribosomes are molecular machines responsible for **protein synthesis**.

They translate information contained in mRNA into a sequence of amino acids.

The simplified process is:

```text
mRNA
 ↓
Ribosome
 ↓
Codons interpreted
 ↓
Amino acids linked
 ↓
Polypeptide
```

Ribosomes themselves contain both RNA and proteins.

---

# 10. Endoplasmic Reticulum

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a membrane-bound network within eukaryotic cells.

It occurs in two major forms.

### Rough ER

Contains ribosomes and is involved in synthesis and processing of many proteins destined for secretion, membranes or specific cellular compartments.

### Smooth ER

Lacks ribosomes on its cytosolic surface and is involved in functions including lipid synthesis and other metabolic processes.

---

# 11. Golgi Apparatus

The Golgi apparatus participates in the modification, sorting and distribution of proteins and lipids.

Proteins synthesized in the secretory pathway can pass through the Golgi apparatus and undergo further processing before being transported to their destinations.

This illustrates an important principle:

```text
Protein synthesis
       ↓
Processing
       ↓
Sorting
       ↓
Specific cellular destination
```

---

# 12. Major Biomolecules

Cells contain several major classes of biomolecules.

The four broad categories commonly discussed are:

* Carbohydrates
* Lipids
* Proteins
* Nucleic acids

Each has different structural characteristics and biological functions.

---

# 13. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are composed primarily of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.

They can function as:

* Energy sources
* Energy-storage molecules
* Structural components
* Recognition molecules

They can be broadly classified into:

### Monosaccharides

Single sugar units.

Examples include:

* Glucose
* Fructose
* Ribose

### Disaccharides

Two monosaccharide units joined together.

Examples include:

* Sucrose
* Lactose
* Maltose

### Polysaccharides

Long chains of sugar units.

Examples include:

* Glycogen
* Starch
* Cellulose

---

# 14. Lipids

Lipids are a diverse group of relatively hydrophobic molecules.

Important examples include:

* Fatty acids
* Triglycerides
* Phospholipids
* Steroids

Lipids have several functions, including:

* Energy storage
* Membrane formation
* Signalling
* Insulation and protection

Phospholipids are particularly important because they form the basic structural framework of biological membranes.

---

# 15. Proteins

Proteins are polymers made from amino acids.

They perform an enormous range of functions, including:

* Catalysis
* Transport
* Structural support
* Cell signalling
* Regulation
* Immune defence
* Molecular recognition

Examples include enzymes, antibodies, receptors, channels and structural proteins.

The wide variety of protein functions arises from differences in amino-acid sequence and resulting three-dimensional structures.

---

# 16. Nucleic Acids

The two major nucleic acids are:

* DNA
* RNA

Their basic functions can be summarized as:

```text
DNA → Genetic information storage

RNA → Information transfer, regulation and functional roles
```

Nucleic acids are particularly important for molecular biology because their sequence can be analysed, amplified, modified and compared.

---

# 17. Biomolecular Interactions

Biological systems depend on interactions between molecules.

Important interactions include:

* Hydrogen bonding
* Ionic interactions
* Hydrophobic interactions
* Van der Waals interactions
* Covalent bonds

For example, DNA base pairing relies on hydrogen bonding, while protein folding depends on a combination of several types of interactions.

These interactions are not equally strong, and their combined effect determines the stability and behaviour of biological molecules.

---

# 18. Enzymes as Biological Catalysts

Many laboratory techniques depend on enzymes.

An enzyme accelerates a chemical reaction by lowering the activation energy required for the reaction.

The molecule acted upon by an enzyme is called its **substrate**.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Enzyme + Substrate
       ↓
Enzyme–substrate complex
       ↓
Product
```

The enzyme itself is not consumed in the overall reaction.

---

# 19. Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

Enzyme activity can be influenced by:

### Temperature

Increasing temperature can initially increase reaction rate, but excessive temperature may disrupt the enzyme's structure and reduce activity.

### pH

Each enzyme has a range of pH conditions in which it functions effectively.

### Substrate concentration

Increasing substrate concentration can increase reaction rate until other factors become limiting.

### Enzyme concentration

Increasing enzyme concentration can increase reaction rate when sufficient substrate is available.

### Inhibitors

Certain molecules can reduce enzyme activity by interacting with the enzyme or affecting substrate binding or catalytic activity.

These principles become particularly relevant when considering enzymes used in PCR, restriction digestion and ligation.

---

# 20. Why Sample Preparation Matters

A biological sample contains many different substances at the same time.

For example, a cell may contain:

```text
DNA
RNA
Proteins
Lipids
Carbohydrates
Ions
Metabolites
Cellular debris
```

If the objective is to study DNA, the DNA must be separated from as many interfering components as practical.

Therefore, a general molecular biology workflow often looks like:

```text
Biological sample
      ↓
Disruption / lysis
      ↓
Release of cellular components
      ↓
Removal of unwanted material
      ↓
Purification
      ↓
Target molecule
```

The exact method depends on the molecule being isolated.

---

# 21. Cell Lysis

**Cell lysis** refers to disruption of the cell so that intracellular components become accessible.

Different organisms and sample types require different approaches.

Lysis can involve combinations of:

* Mechanical disruption
* Detergents
* Enzymatic treatment
* Chemical conditions
* Physical conditions

The objective is not simply to destroy the cell. The method should release the molecule of interest while minimizing unwanted degradation or contamination.

---

# 22. Why DNA Extraction Requires Care

DNA is a relatively stable molecule, but extracted DNA can still be damaged or contaminated.

Potential problems include:

* DNA degradation
* Protein contamination
* RNA contamination
* Salt carryover
* Organic-solvent contamination
* Excessive mechanical shearing

The quality of extracted DNA affects downstream procedures.

For example:

```text
Poor DNA preparation
       ↓
PCR inhibition / poor amplification
       ↓
Unclear downstream results
```

Therefore, DNA isolation and quality assessment should be considered as connected steps.

---

# 23. Why RNA Requires Extra Care

RNA is generally more susceptible to degradation than DNA.

One major reason is the presence of the **2′-OH group** in ribose.

In addition, RNases are widespread environmental and biological contaminants.

Therefore, RNA work commonly requires:

* Clean working conditions
* RNase-free reagents where appropriate
* Appropriate sample handling
* Minimizing unnecessary delays
* Proper storage conditions

This is one reason why RNA electrophoresis and RNA-based experiments require careful sample handling.

---

# 24. Molecular Information Is Encoded in Sequence

For nucleic acids, biological information is represented largely by the order of nucleotides.

For example:

```text
5′-ATGCGTACGCTA-3′
```

is not equivalent to an arbitrary rearrangement of the same bases.

The sequence matters because molecular recognition often depends on the precise nucleotide order.

This principle is central to:

* PCR
* Primer design
* Restriction digestion
* Sequencing
* Genetic identification
* Bioinformatics

---

# 25. From Sequence to Function

A simplified relationship between molecular information and biological function is:

```text
DNA sequence
     ↓
RNA sequence
     ↓
Protein sequence
     ↓
Protein structure
     ↓
Biological function
```

This is a simplified model because many biological processes influence gene expression and protein function, but it provides a useful foundation for understanding molecular biology.

---

# 26. Why Molecular Biology Experiments Need Controls

A result is meaningful only when there is a suitable basis for comparison.

Controls can help determine whether an observed result is actually caused by the experimental variable.

Examples include:

### Positive control

A sample known to produce the expected result.

### Negative control

A sample expected not to produce the target result.

### No-template control in PCR

Contains the PCR reaction components but lacks the intended DNA template.

If amplification occurs in a no-template control, contamination or another source of nonspecific signal needs to be considered.

Controls therefore help distinguish a genuine experimental result from an artefact.

---

# 27. Accuracy, Precision and Reproducibility

These three concepts are important in laboratory work.

### Accuracy

How close a measurement is to the true or accepted value.

### Precision

How closely repeated measurements agree with one another.

### Reproducibility

Whether a result can be obtained consistently under appropriately repeated or comparable experimental conditions.

A laboratory result can be precise but inaccurate if there is a systematic error.

For molecular biology, this is why careful pipetting, correct calculations, proper controls and appropriate equipment settings are important.

# 28. Basic Laboratory Quantitative Thinking

Many molecular biology experiments involve very small quantities and volumes.

Common units include:

### Volume

```text
1 L = 1000 mL

1 mL = 1000 µL

1 µL = 1000 nL
```

### Mass

```text
1 g = 1000 mg

1 mg = 1000 µg

1 µg = 1000 ng
```

### Concentration

Common laboratory units include:

```text
mg/mL
µg/mL
ng/µL
µM
mM
```

Careful unit conversion is essential because an error of a factor of 10 or 1000 can substantially change an experimental reaction.

---

# 29. Concentration and Dilution

The basic dilution relationship is:

```text
C₁V₁ = C₂V₂
```

where:

* `C₁` = starting concentration
* `V₁` = volume of stock solution required
* `C₂` = desired concentration
* `V₂` = final volume

### Example

To prepare 1 mL of a 10 ng/µL DNA solution from a 100 ng/µL stock:

```text
C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

100 × V₁ = 10 × 1000

V₁ = 100 µL
```

Therefore:

```text
DNA stock = 100 µL
Diluent = 900 µL
Final volume = 1000 µL
```

The same equation can be applied to many other laboratory dilution problems, provided the concentration and volume units are used consistently.

---

# 30. Why Unit Consistency Matters

Consider:

```text
100 ng/µL
```

and a desired final volume of:

```text
1 mL
```

The volume should first be converted:

```text
1 mL = 1000 µL
```

Only then should the dilution equation be applied.

A large proportion of simple laboratory calculation errors comes from mixing units without converting them first.

A good habit is:

```text
Write units
      ↓
Convert units
      ↓
Write equation
      ↓
Substitute values
      ↓
Check final units
```

# 32. Key Points to Remember

* Cells are highly organized systems containing many different types of molecules.
* Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells differ in their internal organization.
* Bacteria do not contain a membrane-bound nucleus.
* Eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles.
* DNA, RNA and proteins are central to molecular biology.
* DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides.
* Proteins are polymers of amino acids.
* Enzymes act as biological catalysts.
* Cell lysis releases intracellular components for subsequent analysis.
* Purification is required because biological samples contain many unwanted components.
* RNA generally requires more careful handling because it is more susceptible to degradation.
* Molecular biology depends heavily on accurate measurements and dilution calculations.
* Controls are essential for interpreting experimental results.
* Accuracy, precision and reproducibility are different concepts.
* Good laboratory practice directly affects the quality of experimental data.

# 33. Learning Note

One of the useful connections I made during the training was that laboratory techniques are essentially ways of reducing the complexity of a biological system until the molecule or process of interest can be studied.

A cell contains thousands of different molecular components. If the objective is to study DNA, the experiment has to progressively separate DNA from the rest of that mixture. If the objective is to analyse proteins, the same general idea applies, but the extraction and analysis methods are different because proteins have different chemical properties.

This made the laboratory workflow much easier to understand:

```text
Complex biological system
          ↓
Sample preparation
          ↓
Isolation / purification
          ↓
Quantification
          ↓
Specific molecular analysis
          ↓
Interpretation
```

The practical techniques documented in the following sections can therefore be viewed as different ways of applying this general principle.
