# Central Dogma and Molecular Biology Basics

## 1. Why Molecular Biology Matters

Molecular biology deals with the biological processes that occur at the molecular level, particularly those involving **DNA, RNA, and proteins**.

During the training, I found that many of the laboratory techniques were easier to understand once the relationship between these three molecules was clear. DNA isolation, PCR, gel electrophoresis, recombinant DNA techniques, and protein analysis may look like separate experiments, but they are all connected through the flow of genetic information.

The basic relationship can be represented as:

```text
DNA
 ↓
RNA
 ↓
Protein
```

This is commonly referred to as the **central dogma of molecular biology**.

It is not meant to suggest that information can never move in any other direction. For example, reverse transcription allows information to flow from RNA to DNA.

---

# 2. DNA — The Genetic Material

DNA stands for **deoxyribonucleic acid**. It is the primary hereditary material in almost all organisms.

DNA stores biological information in the form of a sequence of four major nitrogenous bases:

* Adenine (A)
* Thymine (T)
* Guanine (G)
* Cytosine (C)

The sequence of these bases contains the information required for maintaining and reproducing an organism.

A simplified DNA structure can be represented as:

```text
        Sugar–Phosphate Backbone
                 │
5' — A — G — C — T — A — 3'
       │    │    │    │
3' — T — C — G — A — T — 5'
                 │
        Sugar–Phosphate Backbone
```

The two strands are complementary.

Therefore:

```text
A ↔ T
G ↔ C
```

This complementarity is one of the most important principles in molecular biology because it allows DNA to be accurately copied and also forms the basis of techniques such as PCR and nucleic-acid hybridization.

---

# 3. Structure of DNA

DNA is composed of repeating units called **nucleotides**.

Each nucleotide contains three components:

```text
Nucleotide
    │
    ├── Deoxyribose sugar
    ├── Phosphate group
    └── Nitrogenous base
```

The sugar in DNA is **2-deoxyribose**.

The nitrogenous bases are divided into two groups:

### Purines

* Adenine
* Guanine

### Pyrimidines

* Cytosine
* Thymine

The nucleotides are connected through **phosphodiester bonds**, forming the sugar-phosphate backbone.

The two DNA strands are held together mainly by hydrogen bonding between complementary bases.

---

# 4. DNA Double Helix

The classical structure of DNA is a **double helix** consisting of two antiparallel strands.

Antiparallel means that the strands run in opposite directions:

```text
5' ─────────────── 3'
3' ─────────────── 5'
```

The direction of a DNA strand is important because DNA polymerases synthesize new DNA in the **5' → 3' direction**.

This becomes particularly important when understanding PCR.

---

# 5. DNA Replication

DNA replication is the process by which DNA is copied before cell division.

The process is described as **semiconservative replication** because each newly formed DNA molecule contains:

* one original strand
* one newly synthesized strand

A simplified representation is:

```text
Original DNA

        Strand 1
     ─────────────
     ─────────────
        Strand 2

             ↓ replication

      ┌─────────────┐
      │             │
Old + New       Old + New
strand          strand
```

Several proteins and enzymes participate in replication.

Important components include:

### Helicase

Unwinds and separates the two DNA strands.

### Primase

Produces RNA primers required for DNA synthesis.

### DNA Polymerase

Adds nucleotides to the growing DNA strand.

### Ligase

Joins DNA fragments, particularly on the lagging strand.

---

# 6. RNA

RNA stands for **ribonucleic acid**.

Unlike DNA, RNA generally exists as a single-stranded molecule.

The four major bases in RNA are:

* Adenine (A)
* Uracil (U)
* Guanine (G)
* Cytosine (C)

The major difference in base composition is:

```text
DNA → Thymine (T)
RNA → Uracil (U)
```

RNA contains **ribose sugar**, whereas DNA contains **deoxyribose sugar**.

---

# 7. Major Types of RNA

Different types of RNA perform different functions.

### Messenger RNA (mRNA)

mRNA carries genetic information from DNA to the ribosome for protein synthesis.

### Transfer RNA (tRNA)

tRNA brings amino acids to the ribosome during translation.

### Ribosomal RNA (rRNA)

rRNA forms an important structural and functional component of ribosomes.

There are also many regulatory and specialized RNA molecules that participate in gene regulation and other cellular processes.

---

# 8. Transcription

**Transcription** is the process of producing RNA from a DNA template.

A simplified representation is:

```text
DNA
 ↓
RNA polymerase
 ↓
RNA
```

RNA polymerase recognizes appropriate regions of DNA and synthesizes an RNA molecule using one DNA strand as the template.

The RNA strand is synthesized in the **5' → 3' direction**.

For example:

```text
DNA template:
3' — T A C G G A — 5'

RNA:
5' — A U G C C U — 3'
```

The RNA sequence is complementary to the template strand.

---

# 9. Translation

**Translation** is the process by which the nucleotide sequence of mRNA is used to produce a protein.

The process occurs on **ribosomes**.

The basic flow is:

```text
mRNA
 ↓
Ribosome
 ↓
Codons read
 ↓
Amino acids assembled
 ↓
Protein
```

A codon consists of three nucleotides.

For example:

```text
AUG
```

is the commonly recognized start codon and codes for methionine.

The sequence of codons determines the sequence of amino acids in the resulting polypeptide.

---

# 10. Gene Expression

Gene expression refers to the process through which information stored in a gene is used to produce a functional product, usually an RNA or protein.

A simplified pathway is:

```text
Gene
 ↓
Transcription
 ↓
mRNA
 ↓
Translation
 ↓
Protein
```

Gene expression is highly regulated. Cells do not necessarily express every gene at the same level at all times.

This regulation allows different cell types to perform specialized functions even though many of their genes are the same.

---

# 11. Gene, Genome and Chromosome

These terms are related but should not be treated as interchangeable.

### Gene

A gene is a functional region of genetic material that contains information required to produce a functional product or contributes to a biological function.

### Genome

The genome refers to the complete genetic material of an organism.

### Chromosome

A chromosome is a large, organized DNA-containing structure.

The relationship can be simplified as:

```text
Genome
  ↓
Chromosomes
  ↓
DNA
  ↓
Genes
```

The exact organization differs between organisms.

---

# 12. DNA vs RNA

| Feature          | DNA                                   | RNA                                                     |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Full name        | Deoxyribonucleic acid                 | Ribonucleic acid                                        |
| Sugar            | Deoxyribose                           | Ribose                                                  |
| Pyrimidine base  | Thymine                               | Uracil                                                  |
| Common structure | Double-stranded                       | Usually single-stranded                                 |
| Main role        | Long-term genetic information storage | Information transfer, regulation and cellular functions |
| Stability        | Generally more chemically stable      | Generally less chemically stable                        |

The differences in structure explain why DNA is well suited for long-term information storage while RNA is capable of performing a wider range of transient and regulatory functions.

---

# 13. Proteins

Proteins are biological macromolecules composed of **amino acids** linked together by peptide bonds.

Their functions include:

* Enzymatic catalysis
* Structural support
* Transport
* Cell signalling
* Regulation
* Defence
* Molecular recognition

A protein begins as a polypeptide chain, which then folds into a specific three-dimensional structure.

The structure of a protein is closely related to its function.

---

# 14. Levels of Protein Structure

Protein structure is commonly described at four levels.

### Primary Structure

The linear sequence of amino acids.

```text
Amino acid 1 → Amino acid 2 → Amino acid 3 → ...
```

### Secondary Structure

Local structural arrangements such as:

* α-helices
* β-sheets

### Tertiary Structure

The overall three-dimensional structure of a single polypeptide chain.

### Quaternary Structure

The arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits into one functional protein complex.

Understanding these levels becomes particularly useful when studying protein extraction and **SDS-PAGE**, where proteins are treated under conditions that largely disrupt their higher-order structure.

---

# 15. Enzymes

Enzymes are biological catalysts that accelerate chemical reactions without being consumed in the reaction.

Many molecular biology techniques depend directly on enzymes.

Examples include:

| Enzyme                   | Major role                         |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| DNA polymerase           | DNA synthesis                      |
| Restriction endonuclease | DNA cleavage at specific sequences |
| DNA ligase               | Joining DNA fragments              |
| RNA polymerase           | RNA synthesis                      |
| Reverse transcriptase    | RNA-directed DNA synthesis         |

The activity of an enzyme depends on its molecular environment. Temperature, pH, ionic conditions, substrate concentration, and other factors can influence enzymatic reactions.

---

# 16. Complementarity

Base complementarity is a recurring principle throughout molecular biology.

For DNA:

```text
A pairs with T
G pairs with C
```

For RNA-DNA pairing:

```text
A pairs with U
G pairs with C
```

This principle is involved in:

* DNA replication
* Transcription
* PCR primer binding
* Hybridization
* Molecular identification methods

Understanding complementarity makes many molecular biology workflows much easier to follow.

---

# 17. DNA Polymerase and the 5' → 3' Rule

A particularly important point for PCR and DNA replication is that DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to the **3' end of a growing strand**.

Therefore, synthesis proceeds:

```text
5' → 3'
```

This means that the template strand is read in the opposite direction:

```text
Template:
3' → 5'

New strand:
5' → 3'
```

This directional property explains several features of DNA replication and PCR primer design.

# 18. Molecular Biology Techniques and Their Purpose

During the training, several techniques were encountered. Each one answers a different experimental question.

| Technique                          | Main purpose                                        |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Genomic DNA isolation              | Obtain DNA from biological material                 |
| DNA/RNA electrophoresis            | Separate and visualize nucleic acids                |
| DNA quantification                 | Determine DNA concentration                         |
| Quality assessment                 | Evaluate nucleic-acid preparation                   |
| PCR                                | Amplify a selected DNA region                       |
| Primer design                      | Define the target region for PCR                    |
| Restriction digestion              | Cut DNA at specific recognition sequences           |
| DNA ligation                       | Join DNA fragments                                  |
| Transformation                     | Introduce DNA into bacterial cells                  |
| Protein extraction                 | Obtain proteins from biological material            |
| Protein quantification             | Estimate protein concentration                      |
| SDS-PAGE                           | Separate proteins based mainly on molecular size    |
| Molecular microbial identification | Identify microorganisms using molecular information |

This table provides the conceptual link between the basic molecular biology discussed in this section and the practical techniques described in later folders.

# 20. Important Concepts to Remember

The following points form the basic foundation for the practical techniques covered later in this repository:

1. **DNA stores genetic information.**
2. **RNA can carry, regulate, and functionally use genetic information.**
3. **Proteins perform a large variety of cellular functions.**
4. DNA contains A, T, G and C, whereas RNA contains A, U, G and C.
5. Complementary base pairing is central to nucleic-acid biology.
6. DNA synthesis occurs in the **5' → 3' direction**.
7. PCR depends on complementary primer binding and DNA polymerase activity.
8. Restriction enzymes recognize specific DNA sequences.
9. DNA ligase joins compatible DNA ends.
10. Transformation allows foreign DNA to be introduced into suitable bacterial cells.
11. Protein concentration is important when comparing protein samples.
12. SDS-PAGE separates proteins primarily according to molecular size under denaturing conditions.
13. Experimental results need to be interpreted rather than simply recorded.
14. Good laboratory practice is essential for obtaining reliable and reproducible results.

## 21. Personal Learning Note

Before the training, many of these concepts could be understood individually from textbooks. Working through the laboratory techniques made the connections between them much clearer.

For example, DNA isolation is not simply an extraction step. The isolated DNA has to be sufficiently pure for whatever comes next. Its concentration affects how much template is added to a reaction. PCR then selectively amplifies a region of that DNA, and electrophoresis provides a way to examine whether the expected product is present.

Similarly, protein extraction, quantification, and SDS-PAGE form a connected workflow rather than three unrelated techniques.

This connection between **principle → experiment → result → interpretation** is one of the main foundations of the laboratory learning documented in this repository.
