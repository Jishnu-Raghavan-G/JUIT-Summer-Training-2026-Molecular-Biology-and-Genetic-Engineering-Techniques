Molecular Interactions and Stability of Biomolecules
Introduction

Biological molecules do not function simply because of the atoms from which they are made. Their behavior depends heavily on how those atoms interact with one another and with the surrounding environment.

DNA must maintain a sufficiently stable structure to preserve genetic information while still being capable of strand separation during processes such as replication and PCR. Proteins must fold into appropriate three-dimensional structures to perform their functions. RNA must adopt particular structures for many of its biological roles.

These properties are controlled by a combination of covalent bonds, hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces, and interactions with water.

Understanding these interactions is particularly useful in molecular biology because several laboratory techniques deliberately disturb or exploit them.

1. Types of Molecular Interactions

Molecular interactions can broadly be divided into:

Covalent interactions
Hydrogen bonding
Ionic/electrostatic interactions
Hydrophobic interactions
Van der Waals interactions

Their relative importance depends on the molecule and the surrounding conditions.

2. Covalent Bonds

Covalent bonds involve the sharing of electrons between atoms.

They are generally much stronger than the non-covalent interactions involved in maintaining higher-order molecular structures.

Important covalent bonds in molecular biology include:

phosphodiester bonds in DNA and RNA
peptide bonds in proteins
disulfide bonds in some proteins
glycosidic bonds connecting sugars to bases
2.1 Phosphodiester Bonds

Nucleotides in DNA and RNA are connected through phosphodiester bonds.

The phosphate group forms a linkage between:

the 3′ hydroxyl group of one sugar
the 5′ carbon of the next sugar

This produces the sugar-phosphate backbone.

Because of this arrangement, nucleic acids have directionality:

[
5' \rightarrow 3'
]

This direction is extremely important in DNA replication and PCR.

2.2 Peptide Bonds

Amino acids are connected through peptide bonds.

A peptide bond forms between:

the carboxyl group of one amino acid
the amino group of another amino acid

The resulting polypeptide has an N-terminal end and a C-terminal end.

The amino-acid sequence forms the primary structure of a protein.

3. Hydrogen Bonds

Hydrogen bonds are non-covalent interactions involving a hydrogen atom associated with an electronegative atom and another electronegative atom.

They are important in:

DNA base pairing
RNA secondary structure
protein secondary structure
molecular recognition

In DNA:

adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds
guanine pairs with cytosine through three hydrogen bonds

However, DNA stability is not determined only by hydrogen-bond number. Base stacking interactions also make a major contribution to the stability of the double helix.

4. Base Pairing and Complementarity

The complementary nature of DNA allows one strand to act as a template for the other.

The standard base-pair relationships are:

[
A=T
]

[
G=C
]

For example:

5′-A T G C C A T-3′
3′-T A C G G T A-5′

The two strands are complementary and antiparallel.

This principle forms the basis of:

DNA replication
PCR
hybridization
probe-based detection
primer binding
sequencing approaches
5. Base Stacking

Adjacent bases in nucleic acids interact through stacking interactions.

These interactions arise from the arrangement of aromatic bases and their interaction with the surrounding environment.

Base stacking contributes substantially to the stability of double-stranded DNA.

Therefore, describing DNA stability only in terms of hydrogen bonds is incomplete.

This became particularly relevant to me when considering why changes in sequence can influence DNA melting behavior and PCR primer design.

6. Ionic and Electrostatic Interactions

Charged groups attract or repel one another.

For example:

positively charged groups can interact with negatively charged groups
negatively charged groups repel other negatively charged groups

DNA has a negatively charged phosphate backbone.

Therefore, positively charged ions can interact with nucleic acids.

Divalent cations such as Mg²⁺ are especially important in molecular biology.

7. Role of Mg²⁺ in Molecular Biology

Magnesium ions participate in several biochemical processes.

In PCR, Mg²⁺ is required for DNA polymerase activity and interacts with nucleotide triphosphates.

However, the concentration must be appropriate.

Too little Mg²⁺ may result in:

weak amplification
reduced polymerase activity
poor yield

Excessive Mg²⁺ can contribute to:

reduced specificity
nonspecific amplification
unwanted products

Therefore, Mg²⁺ concentration can become an important optimization variable during PCR.

8. Hydrophobic Interactions

Hydrophobic interactions are especially important in protein structure.

Non-polar side chains tend to avoid exposure to water and often become buried within a protein.

This contributes to protein folding.

For example, hydrophobic residues may become concentrated in the interior of a soluble protein while polar and charged residues are more frequently exposed to the aqueous environment.

Hydrophobic effects are therefore an important part of tertiary protein structure.

9. Van der Waals Interactions

Van der Waals interactions are relatively weak interactions that arise from transient or induced charge distributions.

Individually, they are weak.

However, a large number of such interactions can contribute significantly to molecular stability when atoms are positioned appropriately.

Protein folding and molecular recognition therefore depend on the combined effect of many different interactions rather than one interaction acting independently.

10. Protein Folding

A newly synthesized polypeptide does not automatically have its functional three-dimensional structure.

It must adopt an appropriate conformation.

Protein structure is commonly described at four levels.

Primary structure

The amino-acid sequence.

Secondary structure

Local structures such as:

α-helices
β-sheets
Tertiary structure

The complete three-dimensional arrangement of a single polypeptide chain.

Quaternary structure

Association of multiple polypeptide subunits.

11. Forces Stabilizing Proteins

Protein structure can involve:

hydrogen bonds
ionic interactions
hydrophobic interactions
van der Waals interactions
disulfide bonds

The importance of each interaction depends on the particular protein.

Disulfide bonds are covalent and are particularly important in many extracellular proteins.

12. Protein Denaturation

Denaturation refers to disruption of the native structure of a protein.

It can be caused by factors such as:

high temperature
extreme pH
detergents
chaotropic agents
certain organic solvents
reducing conditions for disulfide-dependent structures

Denaturation does not necessarily mean that every covalent bond in the protein has been broken.

Instead, many of the interactions maintaining the higher-order structure are disrupted.

13. SDS and Protein Denaturation

Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) is an anionic detergent commonly used in SDS-PAGE.

SDS interacts with proteins and disrupts many non-covalent interactions.

It also gives proteins a strong negative charge relative to their size.

As a result, during SDS-PAGE, migration becomes much more strongly related to molecular size than it would be for native proteins.

This allows protein samples to be separated according to approximate molecular mass.

14. Reducing Agents

Some proteins contain disulfide bonds between cysteine residues.

Reducing agents such as β-mercaptoethanol or dithiothreitol (DTT) can reduce these disulfide bonds.

This can help separate protein chains that are linked through disulfide bridges.

Therefore, SDS-PAGE sample preparation may involve both:

SDS to disrupt non-covalent structure
a reducing agent when reduction of disulfide bonds is required

The exact treatment depends on the experimental objective.

15. DNA Denaturation

DNA denaturation involves separation of the two complementary strands.

The hydrogen bonding and base-stacking interactions maintaining the duplex are disrupted sufficiently for the strands to separate.

This can be achieved by conditions such as:

increased temperature
extreme pH
certain chemical agents

PCR deliberately uses thermal denaturation.

A typical PCR cycle contains a high-temperature denaturation stage followed by primer annealing and extension.

16. DNA Melting Temperature

The melting temperature ((T_m)) is the temperature at which approximately half of a population of a particular DNA duplex is in the double-stranded state and half is separated under defined conditions.

The actual (T_m) depends on factors including:

sequence
DNA concentration
salt concentration
Mg²⁺ concentration
mismatch
duplex length
neighboring bases

Therefore, there is no single universal (T_m) for a DNA sequence independent of experimental conditions.

17. Simple Primer (T_m) Estimate

For short oligonucleotides, a rough approximation sometimes used is the Wallace rule:

[
T_m\approx2(A+T)+4(G+C)
]

where A, T, G, and C represent the number of each base.

Example

Consider:

ATGCGTAC

Base counts:

A = 2
T = 2
G = 2
C = 2

Therefore:

[
T_m\approx2(2+2)+4(2+2)
]

[
T_m=8+16
]

[
T_m\approx24^\circ C
]

This simple equation is only a rough estimate and should not be treated as an exact experimental melting temperature, particularly for longer primers or precise PCR optimization.

Modern primer-design tools generally use more detailed thermodynamic calculations.

18. GC Content

GC content is another useful sequence property.

It can be calculated as:

[
GC%=
\frac{G+C}{A+T+G+C}\times100
]

Example

For:

ATGCGTAC

there are:

G = 2
C = 2
total bases = 8

Therefore:

[
GC%=\frac{2+2}{8}\times100
]

[
GC%=50%
]

GC content can influence primer melting behavior and hybridization.

However, GC percentage alone is not sufficient to predict primer performance.

19. Salt Concentration and DNA Stability

Ions in solution can affect the behavior of nucleic acids.

The negatively charged phosphate groups on DNA repel one another.

Positive ions partially shield these negative charges.

This can stabilize the double-stranded structure.

Therefore, changes in ionic strength can influence:

DNA melting temperature
primer annealing
hybridization
nucleic-acid structure

This is one reason why PCR conditions cannot be optimized by considering primer sequence alone.

20. Temperature and Biomolecular Stability

Temperature affects molecular interactions and reaction rates.

Increasing temperature can:

increase molecular motion
accelerate many chemical reactions
destabilize nucleic-acid duplexes
disrupt protein structure at sufficiently high temperatures

PCR takes advantage of controlled temperature changes.

The basic cycle is:

Denaturation
      ↓
Annealing
      ↓
Extension
      ↓
Repeat

Each stage is designed around a different molecular requirement.

21. Relationship Between Molecular Interactions and PCR

PCR is a good example of molecular biology being built directly on molecular interactions.

Denaturation

Double-stranded DNA separates.

Annealing

Primers form complementary interactions with their target sequences.

Extension

DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to the primer's 3′ end.

Therefore, successful PCR requires a balance between:

strand stability
primer binding
polymerase activity
ionic conditions
temperature
reagent concentrations
22. Molecular Interactions in Restriction Digestion

Restriction enzymes recognize specific DNA sequences.

Their interaction with DNA depends on molecular recognition between the enzyme and nucleotide sequence.

Once the appropriate recognition site is identified, the enzyme cleaves the DNA at a defined location or within a defined region around that site.

The resulting DNA ends may be:

sticky/cohesive ends
blunt ends

These ends are important for subsequent DNA ligation.

23. Molecular Interactions in DNA Ligation

DNA ligase joins DNA fragments by forming phosphodiester bonds.

Before the covalent bond is formed, complementary DNA ends may associate through base pairing.

For sticky ends:

Fragment A        Fragment B

5′ ───── A T G C
             ││││
3′ ───── T A C G ─────

Complementary sequences bring the DNA fragments into close proximity.

DNA ligase then catalyzes formation of the phosphodiester backbone linkage.

Thus, ligation involves both:

non-covalent base pairing
covalent bond formation
24. Molecular Interactions in Protein Extraction

Protein extraction aims to release proteins while maintaining them in a suitable state for downstream analysis.

Buffer conditions can influence:

protein solubility
protein charge
protein folding
protein-protein interactions
enzyme activity

Detergents may be used to disrupt membranes or solubilize proteins.

Reducing agents may be used when oxidation of cysteine residues or disulfide bonds needs to be controlled.

Therefore, protein extraction is essentially an exercise in controlling the chemical environment around the protein.

25. Water as an Important Biological Environment

Water is not simply a solvent in biological systems.

It strongly influences molecular interactions.

Water molecules interact with:

polar groups
charged groups
hydrogen-bond donors
hydrogen-bond acceptors

The behavior of non-polar groups in water contributes to the hydrophobic effect.

This means that biological molecules are structured partly because of how they interact with the aqueous environment.

26. Denaturation Does Not Mean Complete Molecular Destruction

A common misunderstanding is that denaturation means the molecule has been chemically destroyed.

This is not necessarily true.

For example, when a protein is denatured:

its amino-acid sequence may remain intact
peptide bonds may remain intact
its three-dimensional structure may be disrupted

Similarly, DNA denaturation primarily involves separation of the two strands rather than breaking every covalent bond in the DNA backbone.

This distinction is important when interpreting laboratory procedures.

27. Practical Importance During Laboratory Work

Understanding molecular interactions helps explain several observations that can otherwise appear arbitrary.

For example:

Why PCR needs different temperatures

Different molecular interactions dominate at different stages of the cycle.

Why Mg²⁺ matters

DNA polymerase activity and nucleic-acid interactions depend on appropriate ionic conditions.

Why SDS is used in SDS-PAGE

It disrupts protein structure and provides a strong negative charge.

Why pH matters

Changes in protonation can alter molecular charge and interactions.

Why salt concentration affects DNA

Ionic shielding influences interactions involving the negatively charged DNA backbone.

Why reducing agents are sometimes used

They can break disulfide bonds and alter protein subunit association.

29. Key Takeaways
Biomolecular structure depends on multiple types of interactions acting together.
Covalent bonds form the fundamental chemical framework of DNA, RNA, and proteins.
Hydrogen bonding contributes to nucleic-acid base pairing and protein secondary structure.
Base stacking is an important contributor to DNA duplex stability.
Electrostatic interactions are particularly relevant because DNA has a negatively charged backbone.
Mg²⁺ plays an important role in many molecular biology reactions, including PCR.
Hydrophobic interactions contribute strongly to protein folding.
SDS can disrupt protein structure and provide a strong negative charge for electrophoretic analysis.
DNA denaturation separates strands without necessarily destroying the covalent backbone.
PCR depends directly on controlled changes in molecular interactions.
pH, temperature, salt concentration, and reagent composition can all influence biomolecular stability.
Learning Note

The most useful way for me to understand these interactions is to connect them with the techniques rather than treating them as isolated chemistry topics. PCR, for example, is essentially a controlled manipulation of DNA stability: first the duplex is separated, then primers are allowed to recognize complementary sequences, and finally polymerase extends those primers.

Similarly, SDS-PAGE makes more sense when considered from the perspective of protein structure. SDS changes the physical properties of proteins so that their movement through the gel becomes much more closely related to molecular size.

This connection between molecular interactions and laboratory techniques makes the practical procedures easier to understand instead of simply memorizing them.
